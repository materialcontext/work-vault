# Supplemental Report: Economic Desperation and Resource Extraction in the Path to World War II
## Adam Tooze's *The Wages of Destruction* and Victor Seow's *Carbon Technocracy*

This supplemental report examines two groundbreaking works that fundamentally reshape our understanding of the economic and resource dimensions of World War II's origins. Adam Tooze's *The Wages of Destruction: The Making and Breaking of the Nazi Economy* (2006) revolutionized interpretations of Nazi Germany by demonstrating that Hitler's aggression stemmed from economic weakness rather than strength, while Victor Seow's *Carbon Technocracy: Energy Regimes in Modern East Asia* (2022) reveals how coal extraction at Manchuria's Fushun colliery became central to Japanese imperial expansion and wartime mobilization. Together, these works illuminate how resource scarcity and extraction imperatives drove both European and Asian paths to global conflict.

## Tooze's Revolutionary Reinterpretation: Nazi Germany's Economic Weakness

### The American Challenge and Hitler's Strategic Vision

Adam Tooze's central thesis fundamentally reframes Nazi aggression as a desperate response to American economic hegemony rather than an expression of German strength. **"Wages of Destruction offers a new way of understanding Hitler's aggression as a reaction to a world historic shift – the rise of the United States as the looming hegemon of the twentieth century,"** Tooze argues. For Germans of Hitler's generation, Anglo-American dominance had revealed itself starkly in World War I, creating what Tooze identifies as a double shock: Western victory coupled with Russia's collapse into Bolshevism.

This interpretation challenges conventional narratives by positioning Nazi Germany's military buildup as a race against time before American power became insurmountable. **Hitler's anti-Semitic conspiracy theories directly responded to these shocks, viewing international Jewish finance as the mechanism of Anglo-American global control**. The Nazi regime thus undertook "the greatest and most rapid mobilization of resources in peacetime the capitalist world had ever seen" in a desperate bid to create a European superstate capable of competing with American power.

### Economic Constraints and the Logic of War

Tooze's archival research, summarized in his unpublished working paper "Arming the Reich," reveals the fundamental economic constraints that shaped Nazi decision-making. Rather than the conventional image of an economically powerful Germany bent on conquest, Tooze presents a regime trapped by its own limitations. **Germany's economy remained relatively weak in the 1930s, still requiring significant modernization compared to the United States**. This weakness made Hitler's pursuit of Lebensraum not merely ideological but economically imperative—an attempt to match American continental resources through Eastern European conquest.

The book's analysis of coal and steel production provides the empirical foundation for understanding Nazi motivations. As one reviewer notes, "If you do a word search on this book, I bet you the two first entries will be 'coal' and 'steel.'" **Tooze treats these materials as "the currency in which author Adam Tooze deals in Nazi Germany's motivation, timing and the conduct of WWII, including its worst crimes."** This materialist approach reveals how resource constraints drove strategic decisions previously attributed to ideology alone.

### The Myth of the Armaments Miracle

One of Tooze's most significant contributions involves debunking the myth of Albert Speer's "armaments miracle" and revealing Speer as a committed Nazi rather than an apolitical technocrat. **The book "challenges the idea of an armaments miracle under Albert Speer and rejects the idea that the Nazi economy could have mobilised significantly more women for the war economy."** Instead, Tooze demonstrates that Germany faced insurmountable resource constraints that no amount of reorganization could overcome.

The economic logic of war becomes clear in Tooze's analysis: **"After the Germans had failed to defeat Britain during the Battle of Britain in 1940, the economic logic of the war drove them to Operation Barbarossa, the invasion of the Soviet Union."** Hitler was constrained to invade in 1941 to obtain natural resources necessary to challenge the Anglo-American powers. However, these same resource constraints made victory against the Soviet Union impossible, sealing the Third Reich's fate.

### Strategic Bombing and Economic Warfare

Tooze's reassessment extends to the Allied strategic bombing campaign, arguing for its economic effectiveness while critiquing target selection. **"The book makes the case for the economic impact of the British and then Anglo-American strategic bombing campaigns, but it argues that the wrong targets were often selected."** This nuanced view challenges both those who dismiss strategic bombing as ineffective and those who uncritically celebrate it, instead focusing on its role in Germany's economic strangulation.

## Seow's Carbon Technocracy: The Fushun Colliery as Imperial Lifeline

### Defining Carbon Technocracy

Victor Seow introduces the concept of "carbon technocracy" to describe **"a non-democratic technopolitical system grounded in the idealization of extensive fossil fuel exploitation through mechanical and managerial means."** This framework extends beyond mere resource extraction to encompass "a historical process that is concurrently an alternative account of state formation in modern East Asia and a transnational history of technology."

Building on Timothy Mitchell's *Carbon Democracy* (2011), Seow demonstrates how East Asian carbon extraction precluded the democratic possibilities that emerged in European and American coal regions. **Where Mitchell showed how coal miners in the West could leverage their position in energy flows to demand political rights, Seow reveals how technocratic management in East Asia prevented such mobilization**, creating authoritarian energy regimes that persisted across different political systems.

### Fushun: The Coal Capital of East Asia

The Fushun colliery in Manchuria serves as Seow's lens for understanding broader patterns of energy extraction and state formation. **By 1915, the mine employed 20,000 workers—over a tenth of Japan's total coal mining workforce—and eventually provided an estimated sixth of all Japanese coal consumption**, including both metropolitan and colonial production. This single site's significance extended far beyond its material output to embody the technocratic vision of fossil-fueled modernity.

Seow's meticulous archival work, drawing on "government and company records, gray literature, travel diaries, oral histories, and private collections of mining engineers from China, Japan, Taiwan, and the United States," reveals how successive regimes—Japanese imperial, Soviet, Chinese Nationalist, and Communist—all embraced similar technocratic approaches to coal extraction. **This continuity across ideologically opposed systems demonstrates the power of carbon technocracy as an organizing principle of modern state formation**.

### Warscapes of Intensification

One of Seow's most innovative concepts is the "warscape of intensification," adapted from historian Christopher Jones's "landscape of intensification." This framework captures how **"aggressor states were...motivated to expand further for access to even more resources and to mine presently held deposits with greater ferocity."** This vicious cycle not only relied on gross exploitation of colonized labor forces but also rendered the empire's energy supply routes vulnerable to counterattack.

The wartime mobilization of Fushun exemplifies this process. **Under Japanese control during World War II, the mine became central to the empire's "total" mobilization, with production targets constantly escalating even as conditions deteriorated**. By war's end, Fushun was "but a shadow of its former self, exhausted by the demands of wartime mobilization and the limits of carbon technocracy."

### Technology, Labor, and Violence

Seow's analysis reveals the fundamental contradiction at the heart of carbon technocracy: despite idealization of mechanization and labor-saving technology, coal extraction remained brutally dependent on human bodies. **"As much as these mine operators idealized the might of fossil fuel–driven machines, their extractive efforts nevertheless relied heavily on the human labor that those devices were expected to displace."**

The book opens with the 1932 Pingdingshan massacre, where Japanese forces murdered three thousand Chinese civilians, grounding the technological history in human suffering. Throughout, Seow maintains this "humanist approach and compassionate attitude in unpacking the rise of carbon technocracy," never allowing technical details to obscure the human costs of extraction.

## Converging Insights: Resource Scarcity and Imperial Expansion

### Parallel Structures of Desperation

Both works reveal how resource constraints created similar dynamics in Germany and Japan despite their different contexts. **Tooze shows Germany trapped by its continental position and lack of overseas resources, while Seow demonstrates Japan's parallel predicament as a resource-poor island nation dependent on imperial extraction**. In both cases, the perceived solution—territorial expansion to secure resources—ultimately exacerbated the very vulnerabilities it aimed to address.

The timing of expansion in both theaters reflected these constraints. Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941 was driven by the need to secure resources before American power became overwhelming. Similarly, Japan's intensification of Manchurian extraction and eventual Pacific expansion stemmed from resource anxieties that made continued exploitation appear as the only path to security.

### Technocratic Solutions and Their Failures

Both Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan embraced technocratic approaches to their resource problems, believing that scientific management and technological innovation could overcome material limitations. **Tooze details Nazi Germany's synthetic fuel programs and Four Year Plan, while Seow shows how Japanese engineers pioneered open-pit mining techniques at Fushun**. Yet both cases demonstrate technocracy's ultimate failure when confronted with fundamental resource scarcity.

The faith in technological solutions created dangerous feedback loops. Germany's synthetic fuel production consumed enormous resources while never achieving self-sufficiency. Japan's intensified extraction at Fushun degraded the mine's productivity while increasing dependence on vulnerable supply lines. **Both regimes' technocratic ideologies prevented recognition of these contradictions until military defeat made them undeniable**.

### The Human Cost of Resource Imperatives

Both scholars emphasize how resource extraction imperatives translated into massive human suffering. Tooze connects Nazi food policies directly to the Holocaust, showing how perceived resource scarcity justified genocidal policies. **"The section on the holocaust and the German food problem is phenomenal as well as his connection of Hitler's view of a 'world Jewish conspiracy' to the Führer's economic and foreign policy goals,"** notes one reviewer.

Seow similarly grounds his technological history in human experience, from the Pingdingshan massacre to the systematic exploitation of Chinese labor. **The concept of carbon technocracy explicitly includes the "invasive techniques of labor control, ever-escalating output targets, and the dangers of an increasingly exploited earth"** that characterized resource extraction across political systems.

## Contemporary Relevance and Theoretical Implications

### Beyond Technocratic Solutions

Both works carry urgent contemporary implications for understanding current resource and climate crises. Seow explicitly positions his work as relevant to climate change, with reviewers noting: **"Seow's book arrives as the climatic effects of fossil fuel consumption have become alarmingly apparent everywhere."** His demonstration that carbon technocracy characterized not just Imperial Japan but also Nationalist and Communist China suggests the persistence of these patterns.

The critique of technocratic approaches resonates particularly strongly given current debates over technological solutions to climate change. **"Carbon Technocracy provides reasons to doubt technocracy" as a solution to energy transitions**, suggesting that the same ideological frameworks that drove destructive extraction continue to shape contemporary responses to environmental crisis.

### Rethinking Power and Resources

Tooze's reframing of Nazi aggression as stemming from weakness rather than strength offers crucial insights for understanding contemporary conflicts over resources. **The notion that perceived scarcity and relative decline can drive aggressive expansion remains highly relevant** in an era of resource competition and shifting global power dynamics.

Similarly, Seow's concept of carbon technocracy provides a framework for understanding how energy regimes shape political systems. The continuity of technocratic approaches across ideologically different regimes suggests deep structural forces that transcend particular political arrangements.

## Methodological Contributions

### Materialist Analysis Without Reductionism

Both works demonstrate sophisticated materialist analysis that avoids economic determinism. **Tooze emphasizes "the irreducible autonomy of political ideology" even within his materialist framework**, showing how Hitler's racial worldview shaped economic decisions in ways that pure economic logic cannot explain. The interaction between material constraints and ideological commitments creates the complex dynamics that drove Germany toward war.

Seow similarly balances attention to material extraction with analysis of ideological frameworks and human experience. His concept of carbon technocracy encompasses both physical processes of coal mining and the ideological systems that justified and organized extraction.

### Transnational and Comparative Approaches

Both scholars employ transnational frameworks that reveal connections obscured by national histories. **Tooze positions German developments within a global context of American rise and Anglo-American hegemony**. His attention to how Germans perceived and responded to global shifts provides crucial context for understanding Nazi policies.

Seow's work is explicitly transnational, tracing the circulation of mining expertise and technology between the United States, Germany, Japan, and China. This approach reveals how carbon technocracy emerged not from any single national context but from global circulation of ideas and techniques.

## Conclusion: Resource Scarcity and the Roads to War

Together, *The Wages of Destruction* and *Carbon Technocracy* fundamentally reshape our understanding of World War II's origins by centering resource constraints and extraction imperatives. Both works demonstrate how perceived scarcity drove expansion, how technocratic ideologies promised solutions but delivered destruction, and how human suffering resulted from the pursuit of material resources.

**Tooze's revelation that Nazi aggression stemmed from economic weakness rather than strength parallels Seow's demonstration of how resource anxieties drove Japanese imperial expansion**. Both cases show how the pursuit of autarky through territorial expansion created the very vulnerabilities it aimed to solve, ultimately leading to catastrophic war and defeat.

These works' contemporary relevance extends beyond historical understanding. As the world grapples with climate change and resource competition, the failures of technocratic approaches to resource scarcity carry urgent warnings. **The tendency to seek technological and territorial solutions to systemic problems, the failure to recognize material limits, and the human costs of extraction remain pressing issues**. Understanding how these dynamics drove the world to its most destructive war provides essential insights for avoiding similar catastrophes in our own era of environmental crisis and resource constraint.
