Class 1: Building an Empire - Rome's Mediterranean Transformation (201-59 BCE)
Central Question: How did Rome transform from a regional Italian power into a Mediterranean empire, and what were the consequences of this success?
Part I: The Empire Takes Shape (35 minutes)
A. From Victory to Dominion: Systematic Conquest (12 minutes)
The Logic of Expansion:

Spain (197 BCE): From Carthaginian territories to Roman provinces - silver mines funding further expansion
Macedonia & Greece (146 BCE): Corinth's destruction ending Hellenic independence
Africa (146 BCE): Carthage's annihilation creating Rome's "breadbasket"
Asia (129 BCE): Pergamene inheritance bringing unprecedented wealth (300M sesterces annually)
Eastern Expansion (66-62 BCE): Pompey's creation of 6 new provinces, 12.2M new subjects

Key Insight: Each conquest created both opportunities and necessities for further expansion
B. The Physical Empire: Infrastructure Revolution (12 minutes)
Connecting the Mediterranean:

Road Networks: 13,600km (Italy) → 85,000km (empire) enabling 800km/day official communication
Engineering Mastery: Concrete from pozzolana ash, cambered surfaces, relay stations every 20-30 miles
Urban Development: Military camps (castra) as administrative/commercial hubs
Port Systems: Ostia expansion, strategic harbors integrating sea/land transport

Strategic Purpose: Roads followed military rather than geographic logic - straight lines for rapid troop movement
C. Economic Integration: The Wealth Revolution (11 minutes)
Regional Specialization:

Spain: 80M sesterces annually from mining, Las Médulas alone produced 5M Roman pounds of gold
Africa: Grain surplus feeding Rome's 1M population (world's first megacity)
Asia: Luxury goods hub and banking center
Gaul: Iron, lead, manufactured goods (Terra Sigillata pottery)

Financial Innovation: Publicani tax-farming, argentarii banking, societates investment partnerships
Part II: Military Revolution and Its Consequences (40 minutes)
A. Creating the Professional Army (15 minutes)
Marian Transformation (107 BCE):

End of Citizen-Militia: Property requirements eliminated, landless masses recruited
Career Soldiers: 16-25 year service terms with state equipment and pay
Technological Innovation: Ballista artillery (300m range), siege engines, mobile weapons
Logistical Superiority: Multi-modal supply systems enabling multi-year continental campaigns

The Fatal Trade-off: Military efficiency vs. political loyalty to state
B. Caesar's Gallic Wars: Empire's Apex and Crisis (15 minutes)
Military Genius Demonstrated:

Engineering Feats: Rhine bridge (10 days), Alesia double circumvallation (20km fortifications)
Scale of Conquest: Territory larger than existing empire, 1M killed, 1M enslaved
Economic Impact: Gallic plunder funding Caesar's political dominance
Strategic Consequences: Atlantic access, Germanic buffer, veteran army loyalty

Geographic Integration: Doubling Roman territories while proving no limits to expansion
C. Constitutional Breakdown: When Success Destroys Systems (10 minutes)
The Institutional Crisis:

Extended Commands: Prorogation violating annual limitations
Professional Loyalty: Veterans following commanders, not state
Resource Concentration: Imperial wealth enabling political domination
Military Coercion: Sulla's precedent making force legitimate political tool

Part III: The Wealth of Nations: Economic Transformation (25 minutes)
A. Regional Economic Networks (10 minutes)
Archaeological Evidence of Integration:

Standardized Shipping: Amphora analysis showing Mediterranean-wide distribution
Currency Systems: Lead isotope analysis proving Spanish silver dependence
Trade Routes: Following former Phoenician/Carthaginian networks
Industrial Production: Weaponry, construction, shipbuilding across provinces

B. Urban Growth and Supply Chains (8 minutes)
Feeding the Empire:

Rome's Megacity: World's first 1M population requiring provincial grain
Supply Networks: African/Egyptian grain, Spanish metals, Asian luxuries
Transportation Systems: Coordinated maritime/overland logistics
Economic Dependencies: Urban survival dependent on provincial stability

C. Social Transformation Through Wealth (7 minutes)
Winners and Losers:

New Elite: Publicani, provincial governors, successful generals
Traditional Displacement: Small farmers, urban poor, veterans
Cultural Change: Greek education, eastern luxury, traditional values under pressure
Political Consequences: Wealth concentration enabling constitutional subversion

Part IV: The Path to Caesar (20 minutes)
A. Institutional Adaptation Failures (8 minutes)
Why Republican Solutions Failed:

Scale Problem: City-state institutions governing continental empire
Communication Delays: 2-4 months with distant provinces requiring autonomous governors
Military Necessities: Continuous campaigns requiring extended commands
Resource Flows: Imperial wealth exceeding traditional controls

B. The Triumvirate: Informal Empire (7 minutes)
Power Beyond Constitution:

Secret Alliance: Caesar, Pompey, Crassus dividing empire privately
Provincial Distribution: 5-year commands violating precedent
Constitutional Bypass: Elections and legislation controlled outside formal institutions
Precedent Set: Private agreements replacing public authority

C. Caesar's Choice: Rubicon and Republic's End (5 minutes)
The Logic of Civil War:

Senate's Ultimatum: Return as private citizen facing prosecution
Caesar's Resources: Gallic wealth and veteran loyalty
Institutional Inadequacy: No mechanism for accommodating imperial success
Historical Inevitability: Republican restraints incompatible with imperial power

Synthesis: Empire Before Emperor (15 minutes)
Answering the Central Question (10 minutes)
How Success Destroyed Its Creator:

Military Excellence: Professional armies enabling unlimited expansion
Economic Integration: Wealth flows concentrating power while creating dependencies
Geographic Scale: Continental territories requiring extraordinary authority
Constitutional Breakdown: Each adaptation weakening traditional restraints

Course Connections (5 minutes)
Comparative Analysis:

vs. Persian Empire: Centralized vs. delegated authority
vs. Hellenistic Kingdoms: Personal vs. institutional legitimacy
vs. Carthaginian Networks: Territorial vs. commercial expansion
Setting Up Augustus: Imperial problems requiring imperial solutions
