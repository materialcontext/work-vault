🗳️ Jacksonian Democracy: The Revolutionary Spirit Continues (1828-1840)

Democratic Revolution Accelerates

Expansion of Democratic Participation

🗳️ Suffrage Extension

Property requirements eliminated: Most white men can vote by 1830
Political participation: Voter turnout increases from 25% to 80%
Popular politics: Mass rallies, parades, campaign songs

👥 Popular Political Culture

Common man ideology: Rejection of elite privilege
Party organization: Democratic and Whig parties mobilize voters
Mass media: Newspapers spread political ideas

🏛️ Democratic Ideology

Equality principle: All (white) men created equal in practice
Limited government: Suspicion of concentrated power
Majority rule: Popular will should govern

⚖️ Jackson's Presidency

Spoils system: Common men can hold office
Bank War: Destroys "aristocratic" national bank
Indian removal: Democratic majority overrides minority rights

Connection to Our Course Themes

Remember Week 3's colonial democratic experiments? Jacksonian democracy fulfills the promise of broader political participation that began in colonial assemblies - but for white men only

The Democratic Paradox: Expansion of democracy for white men makes exclusion of enslaved people more glaring and morally indefensible - how can a democratic society based on equality maintain slavery?

🏭 Industrial Revolution Creates Two Different Americas

Economic Development Deepens Sectional Divisions

🏭 Northern Industrial Society

  • Factory system: Mechanized production concentrates workers in cities
  • Wage labor: Free labor ideology celebrates individual opportunity
  • Immigration: European immigrants provide industrial workforce
  • Transportation: Canals and railroads connect markets
  • Education: Public schools and literacy spread
  • Reform movements: Abolition, temperance, women's rights
  • Economic diversity: Manufacturing, commerce, agriculture

🌾 Southern Agricultural Society

  • Plantation system: Large-scale cotton production for export
  • Slave labor: 4 million enslaved people by 1860
  • Cotton gin revolution: Makes cotton extremely profitable
  • Limited industry: Few factories or cities
  • Planter elite: Small group controls wealth and politics
  • Defensive culture: Justifies slavery as "positive good"
  • Economic specialization: Depends on Northern industry and finance

Connecting Back to Week 6: Atlantic Slave Trade

The "revolutionary economic system" of Atlantic slave trade reaches its peak - cotton cultivation makes slavery more profitable and entrenched than ever before

Economic Integration Creates Political Conflict: The more economically connected North and South become, the more their different labor systems create irreconcilable political tensions

🚂 Transportation Revolution: Connecting America, Intensifying Conflict

The Market Revolution Transforms American Life

🚢 Canal System
Erie Canal (1825): Connects Great Lakes to Atlantic
Economic impact: Reduces shipping costs 95%
Regional growth: New York becomes commercial center
🚂 Railroad Expansion
Rapid growth: 30,000 miles of track by 1860
Time compression: Days of travel become hours
National markets: Regional specialization possible
📬 Communication
Telegraph system: Instant long-distance communication
Newspapers: National news spreads rapidly
Political mobilization: Ideas travel faster than ever

The Contradiction of Connection

📈 Economic Integration

North and South become more economically interdependent than ever before

⚔️ Political Division

Better communication spreads sectional arguments faster and more widely

🗺️ Territorial Pressure

Railroads make western territories accessible, forcing slavery question

Historical Parallel: Just as global trade networks in Week 6 spread slavery worldwide, transportation revolution spreads the slavery debate nationwide - technology intensifies rather than resolves political conflicts

⚖️ Missouri Compromise (1820): The "Fire Bell in the Night"

Territorial Expansion Forces the Slavery Question

The Crisis and Its Resolution

🔥 The Crisis
Missouri applies for statehood as slave state; would upset balance of 11 free vs 11 slave states in Senate
📜 The Solution
Maine enters as free state; Missouri as slave state; 36°30' line divides future territories
⚖️ The Balance
Maintains sectional balance in Senate; slavery prohibited north of Missouri's southern border
Thomas Jefferson on Missouri Compromise (1820):
"This momentous question, like a fire bell in the night, awakened and filled me with terror. I considered it at once as the knell of the Union."

Why This Crisis Matters for Our Course

🏛️ Constitutional Limits

First major test of whether constitutional system can handle sectional conflict

🌍 Territorial Expansion

Like European expansion in Week 1, American expansion creates new conflicts

⏰ Temporary Solution

Compromise delays but doesn't resolve fundamental contradiction

The Pattern Emerges: Each attempt to expand slavery into new territories creates national crisis - territorial growth that built American power now threatens American unity

✊ Abolition Movement vs. Pro-Slavery Ideology

Democratic Expansion Empowers Both Sides

The Moral and Political Battle Intensifies

✊ Abolition Movement
  • Moral argument: Slavery violates Christian principles and natural rights
  • Democratic argument: Slavery contradicts Declaration of Independence
  • Economic argument: Free labor more efficient than slave labor
  • Key leaders: William Lloyd Garrison, Frederick Douglass, Sojourner Truth
  • Political strategy: Petitions, newspapers, Underground Railroad
  • Growing support: Northern churches, women's groups join movement
🏛️ Pro-Slavery Response
  • "Positive good" theory: Slavery benefits both races
  • Constitutional argument: Property rights protected by Constitution
  • Economic necessity: Cotton economy requires slave labor
  • Racial ideology: White supremacy justifies enslavement
  • Political defense: Gag rule, fugitive slave laws
  • Southern unity: Non-slaveholders support system

Democratic Participation Intensifies the Conflict

As more white men gain political voice through Jacksonian democracy, both abolitionists and pro-slavery advocates can mobilize larger constituencies - democracy amplifies rather than moderates sectional tensions

Revolutionary Principles vs. Revolutionary Economics: The egalitarian ideals from Week 9 clash directly with the profitable slave system from Week 6 - compromise becomes increasingly impossible

The Success Trap

🏛️ Constitutional Success

Federal system works so well it expands rapidly westward, forcing slavery question repeatedly

💰 Economic Success

Both Northern industry and Southern agriculture thrive, making each section more committed to its system

🗳️ Democratic Success

Political participation spreads so widely that moral arguments reach mass audiences

Setting Up Our Final Class: By 1850, the very success of American revolutionary principles makes the slavery contradiction impossible to ignore or compromise away - a second revolution becomes inevitable
