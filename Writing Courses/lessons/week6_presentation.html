Research Question Development Example

1Broad Interest: "Social media and teenagers"

2Focused Topic: "Social media's impact on teenage mental health"

3Specific Question: "How does Instagram use specifically affect teenage body image and self-esteem among high school students?"

4Arguable Thesis: "Instagram's emphasis on visual perfection significantly damages teenage body image through constant social comparison, algorithmic amplification of unrealistic standards, and the psychological pressure of public validation, requiring immediate intervention in school social media education programs."

❌ Too Broad

"How has technology affected society?"

Problems: Impossible to research comprehensively, no clear argument direction, too many variables

✅ Appropriately Focused

"How has smartphone adoption affected face-to-face conversation skills among American teenagers from 2010-2020?"

Strengths: Specific technology, defined population, clear timeframe, measurable outcomes

Question Refinement Criteria

1Researchable: Sources exist to investigate the question

2Arguable: Reasonable people could disagree about the answer

3Significant: The answer matters to academic or social understanding

4Manageable: Can be thoroughly addressed in your page limit

🧩 Synthesis vs. Patchwork Writing

❌ Patchwork Writing

"According to Smith, social media affects teenagers negatively (Smith 45). However, Johnson argues that social media has positive effects (Johnson 23). Williams also studied social media and found mixed results (Williams 67). Therefore, social media has both positive and negative effects."

Problems: Sources don't talk to each other, no original analysis, just stringing quotes together

✅ True Synthesis

"While Smith's research demonstrates clear psychological harm from excessive social media use, particularly among vulnerable teenagers (Smith 45), Johnson's contrasting findings reveal benefits primarily in carefully moderated contexts with strong adult guidance (Johnson 23). Williams's longitudinal study helps resolve this apparent contradiction by showing that outcomes depend heavily on usage patterns, with passive consumption proving harmful while active community engagement showing positive effects (Williams 67). This suggests that intervention strategies should focus on changing how teenagers use social media rather than simply limiting access."

Strengths: Sources inform each other, original analysis emerges, creates new understanding

Synthesis Writing Strategies

1Compare sources: How do different experts agree or disagree?

2Find patterns: What themes emerge across multiple sources?

3Identify gaps: What questions do sources leave unanswered?

4Add analysis: What do you conclude from combining sources?

🎯 Synthesis Practice Exercise

Take three sources on your topic that seem to contradict each other. Write one paragraph that:

  • Explains how they differ and why
  • Finds any common ground between them
  • Develops your own interpretation of the evidence
  • Advances your overall thesis argument

📖 Extended Essay Organization

📄 8-10 Page Paper Structure

  • Introduction (1-1.5 pages): Hook, context, thesis, preview
  • Literature Review (1 page): What scholars have said
  • Body Section 1 (2 pages): First major argument
  • Body Section 2 (2 pages): Second major argument
  • Counterarguments (1 page): Address opposition
  • Conclusion (1 page): Synthesis and implications

📚 12-15 Page Paper Structure

  • Introduction (1.5 pages): Extended context and thesis
  • Literature Review (2 pages): Scholarly conversation
  • Methodology (1 page): Research approach
  • Argument 1 (2-3 pages): First major section
  • Argument 2 (2-3 pages): Second major section
  • Argument 3 (2-3 pages): Third major section
  • Counterarguments (1 page): Opposition and response
  • Conclusion (1.5 pages): Synthesis and significance

🏗️ Section Development Strategy

  • Opening paragraph: Section thesis and preview
  • Evidence paragraphs: 2-4 paragraphs with sources
  • Analysis paragraph: Your interpretation
  • Transition paragraph: Connect to next section

Key: Each section should feel like a mini-essay supporting your main thesis

🔗 Coherence Strategies

  • Consistent terminology: Use same key terms throughout
  • Clear signposting: "First," "Furthermore," "In conclusion"
  • Thesis reminders: Connect back to main argument
  • Section previews: Tell readers what's coming

Goal: Readers never lose track of your overall argument

📊 Page Allocation Guide

15% Intro
60% Body
10% Counter
15% Conclusion

Remember: These are guidelines, not rigid rules. Adjust based on your argument's needs and complexity.

📝 Advanced Citation Mastery

📚 Complex Source Types

  • Multiple authors: Smith, Jones, and Williams
  • Corporate authors: American Psychological Association
  • No author: Use title or abbreviation
  • Same author, multiple works: (Smith, "First Title"; Smith, "Second Title")
  • Indirect sources: (qtd. in Johnson 45)

🌐 Digital and Modern Sources

  • Online articles: Include URL and access date
  • Database sources: Include database name
  • Social media: Platform and post details
  • Interviews: Personal communication format
  • Government documents: Agency and publication info

🎯 Citation Management

  • Zotero: Free citation management software
  • Mendeley: Research organization and citation
  • Citation spreadsheets: Track sources systematically
  • Note-taking systems: Connect citations to arguments
  • Consistency checking: Same format throughout

Citation Strategy for Long Papers

1Cite as you write: Never leave citations for later

2Over-cite rather than under-cite: Better safe than sorry

3Use citation management tools: Avoid formatting errors

4Double-check accuracy: Verify all source information

🎯 Citation Accuracy Challenge

Create a "practice works cited" page with these challenging source types:

  • An article with 4+ authors from an online database
  • A government report with no individual author
  • An interview you conducted via email
  • A social media post relevant to your topic

📂 Managing Multiple Drafts

🔧 Draft 1: Content Focus

  • Goal: Get all ideas and evidence down
  • Don't worry about: Perfect sentences, transitions
  • Focus on: Argument development, source integration
  • Length: Often 20-30% longer than final

Mindset: "Better to have too much than too little"

✨ Draft 2: Structure & Flow

  • Goal: Organize and streamline arguments
  • Revise: Paragraph order, section transitions
  • Cut: Redundant or weak evidence
  • Add: Missing connections and analysis

Mindset: "Make the argument crystal clear"

🎭 Draft 3: Voice & Style

  • Goal: Polish academic voice and language
  • Enhance: Vocabulary, sentence variety
  • Eliminate: Weak language, repetition
  • Strengthen: Topic sentences, conclusions

Mindset: "Make it sound sophisticated and professional"

🔍 Draft 4: Final Polish

  • Goal: Eliminate all errors and inconsistencies
  • Check: Citations, grammar, formatting
  • Verify: Page requirements, assignment criteria
  • Proofread: Read aloud for final review

Mindset: "Perfect presentation of strong ideas"

File Management System

Naming convention: "LastName_ProjectTitle_Draft1_Date"

Backup strategy: Save in multiple locations (computer, cloud, email)

Version control: Never delete earlier drafts until project complete

Comments/notes: Use document comments for revision reminders

🎯 Revision Schedule Example

  • Monday: Complete Draft 2, focus on argument structure
  • Wednesday: Peer review and feedback incorporation
  • Friday: Draft 3 with voice and style improvements
  • Sunday: Final draft with complete citation check

Key: Plan revision time as carefully as writing time!

⚠️ Avoiding Common Research Pitfalls

❌ Research Pitfall: Topic Too Broad

"The Effects of War on Society"

Problem: Could fill multiple books, impossible to research thoroughly in one paper

Solution: Focus on specific war, specific society, specific type of effect

✅ Properly Focused Topic

"How World War II Factory Work Changed Women's Economic Independence in Rural Tennessee Communities, 1940-1950"

Strengths: Specific conflict, defined location, measurable outcome, clear timeframe

🚫 Pitfall: Confirmation Bias

  • Problem: Only seeking sources that support your thesis
  • Result: Weak, one-sided arguments
  • Solution: Actively search for conflicting evidence
  • Benefit: Stronger arguments that address opposition

📚 Pitfall: Source Overload

  • Problem: Collecting sources without analyzing them
  • Result: Patchwork writing, no original thinking
  • Solution: Take detailed notes with your reactions
  • Benefit: True synthesis and original analysis

⏰ Pitfall: Poor Time Management

  • Problem: Leaving everything until the last week
  • Result: Rushed work, superficial analysis
  • Solution: Break project into weekly goals
  • Benefit: Thoughtful development and strong results

🎯 Pitfall: Losing Focus

  • Problem: Thesis drift during research process
  • Result: Unfocused paper with unclear argument
  • Solution: Regularly return to research question
  • Benefit: Coherent, persuasive final argument
Success Mindset: Great research papers emerge from focused questions, diverse sources, systematic analysis, and consistent effort over time. Avoid shortcuts and embrace the process!

📢 Research Presentation Mastery

🎤 10-Minute Research Presentation

  • Hook (1 min): Engage audience with compelling opening
  • Question & Context (2 min): Why this research matters
  • Main Argument (3 min): Your thesis and key evidence
  • Counterarguments (2 min): Address opposition briefly
  • Conclusions (2 min): Significance and implications

📊 Visual Aid Strategy

  • Slide 1: Title and compelling question
  • Slide 2: Research question and thesis
  • Slides 3-5: Key evidence with visuals
  • Slide 6: Counterargument acknowledgment
  • Slide 7: Conclusions and broader significance

🗣️ Delivery Best Practices

  • Eye contact: Connect with your audience
  • Clear speech: Slower than normal conversation
  • Confident posture: Stand tall, use gestures
  • Time management: Practice with timer
  • Q&A preparation: Anticipate audience questions

💡 Beyond the Classroom

  • Student conferences: Present at academic meetings
  • Community presentations: Share with local groups
  • Online publication: Blog posts or school website
  • Contest submissions: Research competitions
  • College applications: Demonstrate academic capability
